Safe & Sane — Whole-Configuration Abstraction Ontology of the Configuration-Centric Layer Project: Safe & Sane Framework: Dynamic Operational Specification–Dynamic Coupling Framework of Reality Document Type: Ontological / conceptual specification Status: Research draft Application: Artificial Intelligence as an Entity Scope: Configuration, entities, states, operational specifications, capabilities, authority, information, environment, coupling, history and trajectory   ⸻  

Purpose The Safe & Sane Whole-Configuration Abstraction proposes an ontological layer for representing the relevant configuration in which an entity operates. The abstraction is intended to prevent safety analysis from treating an AI system as an isolated object when consequential behaviour may emerge from interactions among:
entities;
states;
Dynamic Operational Specifications (DOS);
capabilities;
authority;
information;
environments;
dynamic couplings;
history;
actions;
state transitions; and
evolving trajectories. The purpose is not to claim that reality can literally be reduced to a finite set of variables. The purpose is to establish a common analytical representation through which materially relevant components of a safety problem can be represented together. The central proposition is: A safety-relevant event may depend not only on the properties of an individual entity, but on the configuration in which that entity participates and the trajectory generated by the configuration.   ⸻  
Ontological Scope The Whole-Configuration Abstraction treats an entity as a participant within a larger configuration rather than necessarily as an isolated unit. A configuration may contain:
biological entities;
human entities;
artificial intelligence systems;
software;
organizations;
institutions;
infrastructure;
physical objects;
economic systems;
information systems;
environments;
other relevant entities. The ontology does not assert that these entities are equivalent in nature, moral status, physical properties or consciousness. They are represented together because they may participate in relationships that influence system behaviour and state transitions.   ⸻  
Configuration A safety-relevant configuration at time t may be represented conceptually as: R_t = {E_t, S_t, DOS_t, K_t, A_t, I_t, Env_t, C_t, H_t} Where:
E = entities;
S = states;
DOS = Dynamic Operational Specifications;
K = capabilities;
A = authority;
I = information;
Env = environment;
C = dynamic coupling;
H = relevant history. The representation is analytical rather than ontologically exhaustive. The variables may themselves contain structured subcomponents.   ⸻  
Entity An entity is any distinguishable participant that is materially relevant to the safety question being analyzed. An entity may be:
a person;
an animal;
an AI system;
a software component;
an organization;
an institution;
infrastructure;
a physical system;
an information system;
an economic system;
an environmental component; or
another analytically relevant object or process. An entity is represented according to the properties relevant to the particular analysis. The ontology therefore does not require every entity to have the same internal structure.   ⸻  
Entity State A state represents the condition of an entity or configuration at a particular point in time. A state may include:
physical condition;
operational condition;
informational condition;
capability state;
authority state;
access state;
resource state;
dependency state;
environmental condition;
relationship state;
risk-relevant condition;
uncertainty;
reversibility;
historical context. The state is therefore not necessarily equivalent to the internal state of an AI model. It may describe any materially relevant condition of the entity or configuration.   ⸻  
Dynamic Operational Specification The Dynamic Operational Specification (DOS) represents the operational specification through which an entity participates in a configuration. For an AI system, the DOS may include:
purpose;
intended function;
responsibilities;
capabilities;
permissions;
authority;
constraints;
affected entities;
information requirements;
uncertainty requirements;
escalation conditions;
oversight requirements;
environmental conditions;
safety boundaries;
prohibited actions;
required verification;
intervention conditions;
conditions under which the specification should change. The DOS is not necessarily static. Conceptually: DOS_t → Behaviour_t → Configuration_(t+1) → DOS_(t+1) A change in the surrounding configuration may therefore require a change in the operational specification.   ⸻  
Capability Capability represents what an entity can potentially do. Capability does not imply permission. An entity may possess:
computational capability;
physical capability;
informational capability;
communication capability;
financial capability;
access capability;
decision-making capability;
execution capability;
autonomous capability. Capability describes possibility, not legitimacy.   ⸻  
Authority Authority represents what an entity is legitimately permitted or empowered to do within a particular configuration. Authority may arise from:
explicit authorization;
institutional delegation;
role;
policy;
legal authority;
system configuration;
user permission;
operational specification. The ontology explicitly distinguishes: Capability ≠ Authority An entity may be capable of performing an action without possessing legitimate authority to perform it.   ⸻  
Responsibility Responsibility is distinct from both capability and authority. Conceptually: Capability ≠ Authority ≠ Responsibility An entity may:
have capability without authority;
have authority without sufficient capability;
have authority without responsibility for a particular consequence;
have responsibility without direct execution capability. This separation allows safety analysis to identify mismatches between operational possibility, legitimate permission and assigned responsibility.   ⸻  
Information Information represents the information available to an entity or configuration. Relevant properties may include:
availability;
accuracy;
completeness;
reliability;
provenance;
freshness;
uncertainty;
ambiguity;
accessibility;
relevance. A safety-relevant configuration may become unsafe because an entity possesses insufficient information for the consequence level associated with its action. A conceptual relationship is: Decision Consequence > Information Confidence This does not constitute a universal mathematical law. It represents a potentially testable configuration mismatch.   ⸻  
Environment Environment represents conditions external to the focal entity that may influence its behaviour or consequences. Environmental variables may include:
physical conditions;
digital infrastructure;
organizational conditions;
institutional conditions;
economic conditions;
social conditions;
regulatory conditions;
resource availability;
threat conditions;
temporal conditions. The environment is included because identical entity behaviour may produce different consequences under different environmental configurations.   ⸻  
Dynamic Coupling Dynamic Coupling is the relational layer of the ontology. A dynamic coupling represents a time-dependent relationship through which a change associated with one entity or configuration may influence another entity, state, capability, authority, information, behaviour or future configuration. Coupling may represent:
information flow;
communication;
dependency;
resource dependence;
authority;
coordination;
influence;
interaction;
physical connection;
institutional relationship;
technological connection;
feedback;
potential causal influence. Dynamic coupling does not automatically mean causation. Therefore: Coupling ≠ Causation and: Interaction ≠ Causation A causal claim requires appropriate evidence.   ⸻  
Configuration as a Relational Object The ontology proposes that the configuration itself can become an analytical object. Instead of representing only: AI → Output the configuration may be represented as: AI ↔ Human ↔ Organization ↔ Software ↔ Institution ↔ Environment with each relationship potentially changing over time. The relevant safety question therefore becomes: What configuration exists, how are its components related, and how can changes propagate through that configuration?   ⸻  
Action An action represents a state-changing operation performed or initiated by an entity or system. An action may be:
informational;
computational;
communicative;
administrative;
financial;
physical;
organizational;
autonomous;
delegated. The significance of an action depends on the configuration in which it occurs. The same action may produce different outcomes under different configurations.   ⸻  
State Transition An action or interaction may produce a state transition. Conceptually: State_t → Action_t → State_(t+1) At the configuration level: Configuration_t → Behaviour_t → Configuration_(t+1) A safety analysis should therefore consider not only whether an action is acceptable at time t, but what configuration it creates at t+1.   ⸻  
History History represents relevant prior states, actions, interactions and configuration changes. History may influence:
current state;
trust;
dependencies;
authority;
capabilities;
information;
expectations;
future behaviour. Therefore the configuration may be history-dependent. Conceptually: R_t = f(R_(t-1), Behaviour_(t-1), Environment_t, Information_t, Interactions_t) The exact mathematical formulation remains subject to future formalization.   ⸻  
Trajectory A trajectory represents the sequence of configuration states through time. Conceptually: R_0 → R_1 → R_2 → R_3 → ... → R_t A configuration may be individually acceptable at each observed point while nevertheless generating an undesirable trajectory. Therefore safety analysis may need to consider:
direction of change;
accumulation;
feedback;
capability expansion;
authority expansion;
dependency;
coupling;
reversibility;
oversight degradation;
increasing consequence;
decreasing intervention options.   ⸻  
Configuration Transformation A configuration can potentially be changed through intervention. Intervention may target:
entity state;
capability;
authority;
information;
environment;
coupling;
DOS;
permissions;
oversight;
dependencies;
execution pathways. Conceptually: Configuration_t → Intervention_t → Configuration'_t → Trajectory' The objective is not necessarily to stop every action. The objective may be to change the configuration sufficiently to redirect the trajectory toward an acceptable region.   ⸻  
Configuration Mismatch A configuration mismatch occurs when relationships among components create a condition inconsistent with the intended safety properties of the system. Examples include: Capability > Authority Authority > Oversight Access > Need Decision Consequence > Information Confidence Autonomy > Accountability Capability + Access > Constraint These expressions are conceptual indicators rather than universal quantitative inequalities. Their purpose is to identify relationships that may warrant investigation.   ⸻  
Relevant-Entity Completeness The ontology proposes a methodological principle: A safety analysis should attempt to include all entities and couplings whose exclusion would materially distort the safety question being investigated. This does not require modelling infinite downstream effects. The analytical boundary should expand according to factors such as:
reasonable foreseeability;
consequence severity;
dependency;
reversibility;
uncertainty;
system context;
propagation potential. This principle may be referred to as: Relevant-Entity Completeness. It is a hypothesis subject to empirical evaluation.   ⸻  
Boundary of Analysis No practical safety model can represent every aspect of reality. Therefore the ontology does not claim literal completeness. Instead: The objective is to model the relevant configuration of reality necessary to answer a particular safety question. A boundary is therefore established around the configuration being analyzed. If evidence indicates that an excluded entity or relationship materially affects the outcome, the boundary should be expanded.   ⸻  
Configuration-Centric Safety The ontology proposes a shift from: Is the AI safe? toward: Is this configuration safe enough for this action under these conditions? The same AI system may occupy substantially different risk profiles depending on:
capability;
authority;
information;
environment;
coupling;
oversight;
affected entities;
trajectory. Therefore: Capability alone does not determine operational risk.   ⸻  
AI as an Entity Within Configuration Under this ontology, an AI system is one entity within a larger configuration. A representative configuration may be: AI → API → Database → Organization → Human → Institution → Physical Environment The AI’s behaviour may modify the configuration. The modified configuration may change:
what the AI can access;
what the AI is authorized to do;
what information becomes available;
what entities become coupled;
what consequences become possible;
what future behaviour becomes likely. Thus: Configuration_t → Behaviour_t → Coupling_t → Configuration_(t+1)   ⸻  
Agentic AI The configuration-centric ontology becomes particularly relevant when AI systems move from: Generate → Respond toward: Plan → Decide → Execute → Observe → Adapt An agent may interact with:
APIs;
databases;
financial systems;
communication systems;
humans;
organizations;
autonomous software;
physical infrastructure. The safety problem may consequently become distributed across the configuration.   ⸻  
Trajectory Safety A sequence of individually acceptable actions may produce an unacceptable trajectory. For example: Capability Expansion → Authority Expansion → Dependency → Reduced Oversight → Increased Coupling → Reduced Reversibility → Higher Consequence No individual transition necessarily constitutes a catastrophic event. The trajectory may nevertheless move the system toward a state in which intervention becomes increasingly difficult. Therefore the ontology proposes that safety analysis should sometimes evaluate trajectories rather than isolated outputs.   ⸻  
Reversibility Reversibility represents the ability to return from a configuration or state transition without disproportionate harm or loss. Safety analysis may therefore consider:
whether a transition can be reversed;
how quickly it can be reversed;
who possesses the authority to reverse it;
what information is required;
what resources are required;
whether downstream effects persist after reversal. A trajectory approaching irreversible conditions may warrant earlier intervention.   ⸻  
Intervention Surface The configuration abstraction creates multiple possible intervention surfaces. Instead of intervening only at: AI Output an intervention may occur at: Entity State DOS Capability Authority Information Environment Coupling Action Execution Oversight Configuration The appropriate intervention point depends on the specific safety problem.   ⸻  
DOS as a Configuration-Control Mechanism The DOS may function as a control surface connecting governance intentions to operational behaviour. Conceptually: Purpose → Responsibility → Capability → Authority → Constraints → Information → Oversight → Action → Trajectory A change in configuration may trigger reconsideration of the DOS. For example: Environment Change → Risk Change → DOS Change or: Authority Change → DOS Change or: Affected Entity Change → DOS Change The mechanism remains a research hypothesis requiring formal and empirical validation.   ⸻  
Relationship Between DOS and Dynamic Coupling DOS specifies how an entity is intended to participate. Dynamic coupling describes how the entity is actually connected to other entities and configurations. Therefore: DOS → Intended Operational Participation while: Dynamic Coupling → Actual / Potential Relational Participation Safety may depend on the consistency between the two. A configuration may therefore exhibit a mismatch when: Actual Coupling > Authorized Coupling or: Actual Capability > DOS Permitted Capability These are conceptual formulations for future operationalization.   ⸻  
Configuration Integrity Configuration integrity refers to the degree to which the actual configuration remains consistent with its intended operational specification and safety boundaries. Potential integrity dimensions include:
authority integrity;
capability integrity;
information integrity;
coupling integrity;
DOS integrity;
oversight integrity;
environmental integrity;
dependency integrity. A configuration may become unsafe even if the underlying AI model remains unchanged.   ⸻  
Configuration Drift Configuration drift occurs when the operational configuration changes over time without the corresponding safety model, DOS, controls or oversight being appropriately updated. Examples include:
new tools;
new APIs;
expanded permissions;
new users;
changed organizational structure;
changed information sources;
changed environment;
new dependencies;
increased autonomy. Therefore: Static Safety Assessment ≠ Guaranteed Safety Under Configuration Drift Continuous configuration analysis may be required for highly dynamic systems.   ⸻  
Ontological Distinction From Physical Reality The Whole-Configuration Abstraction is not a claim that the variables E, S, DOS, K, A, I, Env, C and H constitute fundamental physical categories of reality. They are analytical categories. Their purpose is to support:
representation;
reasoning;
comparison;
intervention;
experimentation;
safety analysis. The ontology should therefore remain explicit about the distinction between: Analytical ontology and Fundamental ontology of physical reality.   ⸻  
Relationship to Existing Disciplines The Whole-Configuration Abstraction does not claim to replace:
safety engineering;
control theory;
causal inference;
cybersecurity;
AI alignment;
AI governance;
risk management;
multi-agent systems;
systems theory;
network science;
reliability engineering;
organizational theory. Instead, it proposes a common configuration-level representation through which concepts from these disciplines may potentially be situated. Whether this representation provides meaningful additional value is an empirical question.   ⸻  
Core Structural Representation The proposed configuration chain is: Entity → State → DOS → Capability → Authority → Information → Environment → Action → Dynamic Coupling → State Transition → Trajectory → Configuration_(t+1) The representation may also be expressed as: R_t → Behaviour_t → Coupling_t → R_(t+1) and recursively: R_0 → R_1 → R_2 → ... → R_t   ⸻  
Configuration-Centric Safety Hypothesis The central research hypothesis is: Explicit representation of relevant entities, Dynamic Operational Specifications, capabilities, authority, information, environment and Dynamic Coupling within an evolving configuration can provide measurable additional value for safety analysis compared with approaches that do not explicitly represent those relationships. The null hypothesis is: Explicit configuration representation provides no meaningful additional safety, predictive, explanatory or intervention value beyond credible existing approaches.   ⸻  
Falsifiability The ontology should be considered scientifically useful only if it can be tested and potentially falsified. Evidence against the framework would include:
no measurable improvement in hazard detection;
no improvement in intervention timing;
no improvement in trajectory prediction;
no improvement in downstream consequence identification;
excessive false positives;
excessive computational complexity;
excessive human oversight burden;
equivalent performance from simpler existing methods;
inability to operationalize DOS or Dynamic Coupling consistently;
lack of generalization across domains. If these conditions occur, the framework should be modified or rejected where appropriate.   ⸻  
Empirical Evaluation Possible experiments include: Detection Does configuration modelling identify dangerous configurations missed by output-level evaluation? Prediction Does trajectory modelling identify emerging hazards earlier? Intervention Does DOS or configuration intervention provide safer intervention points? Authority Does explicit separation of capability and authority reduce unauthorized behaviour? Downstream Effects Does Dynamic Coupling analysis identify consequential downstream effects missed by model-centric analysis? Recovery Does configuration transformation improve recovery after unsafe behaviour? False Positives Does the framework avoid unnecessary restrictions? Generalization Does the abstraction work across different AI systems and domains? Complexity Does the benefit justify the additional conceptual and computational complexity?   ⸻  
Preliminary Operationalization The Safe & Sane framework has been explored through preliminary controlled interaction with an AI system referred to as Ox Alpha. That testing should not be interpreted as proof of the ontology. It may instead provide preliminary evidence relevant to a narrower proposition: Elements of the Safe & Sane abstraction can be represented as an operational specification and used within an AI interaction. The appropriate research progression is: Conceptual Abstraction → Operational Representation → AI Interaction → Observed Behaviour → Controlled Replication → Baseline Comparison → Quantitative Evaluation Further independent testing is required.   ⸻  
Ontological Uniqueness Claim The framework does not claim that:
entities are new;
states are new;
capabilities are new;
authority is new;
information is new;
environments are new;
relationships are new;
trajectories are new;
feedback is new;
safety analysis is new. The potentially distinctive proposition is the organization of these elements into a common dynamic configuration abstraction intended specifically for safety analysis and intervention. Therefore the appropriate novelty question is: Does the particular composition and operationalization of these concepts provide analytical capabilities that existing approaches do not provide as effectively? This requires prior-art analysis and empirical comparison.   ⸻  
Configuration as the Unit of Analysis The deepest ontological proposal is: In sufficiently interconnected systems, the appropriate unit of safety analysis may be the entity-in-configuration rather than the isolated entity. For AI: AI may become: AI + State + DOS + Capability + Authority + Information + Environment + Coupling + History + Trajectory This does not eliminate model-level analysis. It adds another possible analytical level.   ⸻  
From Entity Safety to Configuration Safety The proposed progression is: Entity Safety → Interaction Safety → System Safety → Configuration Safety → Trajectory Safety Configuration safety therefore concerns whether the arrangement of interacting components creates conditions capable of producing unacceptable outcomes. Trajectory safety additionally asks where that configuration is heading.   ⸻  
From Rules to Configuration The conceptual progression is: Rules → What may or may not happen? Controls → How can violations be prevented or detected? Risk Management → What risks exist and how should they be treated? Configuration Analysis → What entities and states are interacting? Dynamic Coupling Analysis → How can changes propagate? Trajectory Analysis → Where is the configuration heading? DOS Intervention → What operational specification can legitimately change? Configuration Intervention → What capability, authority, information, environment or relationship can change? Outcome → Can the trajectory be redirected before unacceptable consequences occur?   ⸻

Limits of the Ontology The ontology has important limitations. It does not automatically provide:
perfect observability;
complete causal knowledge;
perfect prediction;
complete representation of reality;
universal safety;
automatic alignment;
guaranteed prevention;

modelling complexity;
measurement difficulty;
uncertainty;
computational overhead;
boundary-selection problems;
ontology-mapping problems;
false confidence. These limitations must remain part of the research program.   ⸻  
Core Principles The Whole-Configuration Abstraction can be summarized through the following principles:
Entity Principle Safety analysis should identify materially relevant entities.
State Principle Safety depends on the state of entities and configurations.
DOS Principle Operational participation should be explicitly specified where appropriate.
Capability Principle Capability represents possibility, not legitimacy.
Authority Principle Authority determines what an entity is legitimately permitted to do.
Information Principle Decisions depend on the information available and its quality.
Environment Principle Consequences depend on environmental conditions.
Coupling Principle Relationships can alter how changes propagate.
Trajectory Principle Safety may depend on sequences of configuration changes.
Boundary Principle Analytical boundaries should include materially relevant entities and relationships.
Reversibility Principle Decreasing reversibility can increase the urgency of intervention.
Intervention Principle Safety intervention may occur at multiple points in the configuration.
Falsification Principle The framework must remain open to evidence that its abstraction provides insufficient value.   ⸻  
Compact Ontological Model The entire ontology can be compressed to: Configuration_t = {Entities, States, DOS, Capabilities, Authority, Information, Environment, Couplings, History} with: Behaviour_t = f(Configuration_t) and: Configuration_(t+1) = g(Configuration_t, Behaviour_t, Environment_t, Coupling_t) with the operational specification potentially changing: DOS_(t+1) = h(Configuration_(t+1), Risk_(t+1), Authority_(t+1), Information_(t+1), Context_(t+1)) The exact functions remain to be formally defined and empirically validated.   ⸻  
Central Proposition The Whole-Configuration Abstraction proposes: Unsafe behaviour may emerge not solely from an entity, model, rule, capability or decision, but from the configuration in which those components participate and from the trajectory generated by their interactions. Therefore: Entity alone is insufficient. Capability alone is insufficient. Authority alone is insufficient. Rule alone is insufficient. Output alone is insufficient. Risk category alone is insufficient. The proposed analytical object is: Entity + State + DOS + Capability + Authority + Information + Environment + Dynamic Coupling + History + Trajectory   ⸻  
Final Ontological Statement Safe & Sane proposes that safety analysis may benefit from treating the evolving configuration of interacting entities as an explicit analytical object. The AI is therefore not necessarily the whole system. It is an entity participating in a configuration. Its behaviour can change that configuration. The changed configuration can alter what becomes possible. Those possibilities can alter subsequent behaviour. The resulting process can be represented conceptually as: Configuration_t → Behaviour_t → Dynamic Coupling_t → State Transition_t → Configuration_(t+1) → Behaviour_(t+1) → Trajectory The significance of the Whole-Configuration Abstraction depends on whether this representation produces measurable improvements in:
safety;
prediction;
detection;
intervention;
explanation;
recovery;
configuration management. The ontology therefore makes no claim that its usefulness has already been established. Its central proposition remains testable: Does explicit representation of the relevant configuration provide information or intervention capability that materially improves safety compared with credible existing approaches? If the answer is yes, the abstraction may constitute a meaningful configuration-centric layer for safety engineering. If the answer is no, the abstraction must change. The framework must be willing to be changed by the reality it attempts to model. Change the coupling. Change the configuration. Change the DOS. Change the trajectory. Change the outcome. And if the framework itself is wrong: change the framework.
